Learning objectives

By the end of this episode you will be able to

  1. explain the three limits of what a model knows, and what RAG adds: a library card for a scholar who graduated years ago
  2. choose between RAG, fine-tuning and long context for a given task
  3. describe the two RAG pipelines: indexing once, and retrieve-then-generate for every question
  4. use embeddings and cosine similarity to find the top-k chunks for a question
  5. write a prompt that makes the model answer only from the documents, cite them, and say when they don’t contain the answer
  6. compare sparse (BM25) and dense retrieval, and merge them with Reciprocal Rank Fusion
  7. choose a chunking strategy (fixed size with overlap, structure-aware, parent-child) and measure it, instead of guessing

Can the model answer from our book?

In EP09 Lab 3 we asked about a paper in our library, and the model never opened it: it answered from the title and what it already knew. The answer sounded fine, but we had no idea where it came from. This episode starts module M3 · RAG with one question: how do we make the model really answer from our own documents, and tell us which page each answer came from?

Three limits, two kinds of knowledge

A model stores its knowledge in its weights, compressed during training. The textbook names three limits that come with this (Roitman 2026, sec. 16.1):

  1. Hallucination (EP02): asked beyond what it reliably knows, it still answers confidently.
  2. Staleness: the training data has a cutoff date. Our textbook came out in 2026, after our model was trained.
  3. Domain specificity: a general model has never seen your company’s documents, your university’s regulations or your code.

Knowledge in the weights is parametric; knowledge in documents outside the model is non-parametric. The textbook’s picture: a plain model is a scholar who memorized an enormous library but graduated years ago. RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) gives that scholar a library card: look things up, cite the source, and admit when a reference needs checking instead of guessing from memory.

In Lab 1, asked without RAG which analogy the book uses to explain RAG, Gemma answered confidently: “an open-book exam”. The phrase is on no page of the book. With RAG it answered “the library card”, citing Doc 2, page 295.

RAG, fine-tuning or long context

RAG is not a replacement for fine-tuning: fine-tuning teaches the model how to reason and respond; RAG provides what to reason about. Like training a chef, and delivering fresh ingredients every morning.

Two pipelines: index once, answer any question

Keep the metadata (which file, which page): it is what lets the answer cite its source later. Like a library, the catalogue cards are made once, when a book arrives, and then used for everyone who comes to ask.

Embeddings and cosine similarity

An embedding model turns a text into a list of numbers that stands for its meaning; ours returns 4,096. Texts with similar meanings get vectors that point the same way. Cosine similarity measures how much two vectors point in the same direction, and top-k keeps the k chunks with the highest score for a question.

In Lab 1 car·automobile scored ≈ 0.95, but car·banana still scored ≈ 0.58, not zero. Unrelated texts often score fairly high, so what we really use is the order, not a single number.

Generation: answer only from the documents

Listing 16.1 gives a prompt with three rules: answer using only the provided context; if the context isn’t enough, say so explicitly; cite sources as [Doc n]. Each chunk in the prompt is labelled with its Doc number, its file and its page. “The documents don’t say” is not a failure: it is exactly what we want.

In Lab 1, asked for the capital of Australia, the model said the context doesn’t contain it, even though it knows the answer. Our library doesn’t mention Australia, so it didn’t answer from memory.

Sparse, dense and hybrid retrieval

Sparse retrieval scores a chunk by the query’s words it contains, like the index at the back of a book. The most widely used is BM25: rare words weigh more, repeating a word helps but levels off, and very long chunks lose a little. It wins on error codes, proper nouns and rare or new terms, needs no GPU, and is easy to explain, but “car” won’t find “automobile”.

Dense retrieval uses embeddings (the textbook’s example is DPR, with one encoder for questions and one for passages). It finds meaning: ask how to split a long document, and it finds the chunk about chunking. At millions of chunks the search is approximate (for example with FAISS). Table 16.2 compares more methods; the cross-encoder is the most accurate but slow, so it re-ranks chunks already retrieved (EP11).

Hybrid uses both and merges the results. The two scores are on different scales, so the textbook uses Reciprocal Rank Fusion, which looks only at ranks:

\[\text{RRF}(d) = \sum_{\text{lists}} \frac{1}{60 + \text{rank}(d)}\]

Rank 3 in BM25 and rank 7 in dense gives 1/63 + 1/67 ≈ 0.0308; first in both gives ≈ 0.0328. The 60 keeps the top ranks from dominating.

In Lab 2 we wrote BM25 and RRF ourselves, and RRF matched the book’s 0.0308 and 0.0328. “What is PLAID?”: BM25 found it at rank 1, dense around rank 99. Asked about parent-child chunking in other words, dense found pages 302–303 and BM25 missed them. On 10 questions, hit@3 was BM25 4, dense 4, hybrid 8. Better overall, but not on every question: two got worse.

Chunking

The textbook calls chunking one of the highest-impact decisions in a RAG system (Roitman 2026, sec. 16.4). A good chunk is small enough for the embedding model, about one topic, and makes sense on its own.

  • Fixed size with overlap: cut every W tokens, neighbours sharing O tokens, so a sentence cut at a boundary is whole in one of them. A 1,000-token document with W = 512 and O = 64 gives ⌈(1000 − 64) / (512 − 64)⌉ = 3 chunks (Equation 16.19).
  • Semantic: cut where neighbouring sentences stop being similar.
  • Structure-aware: split at headers, functions or classes, and never split a table mid-row.
  • Parent-child: search with small chunks (precise), but send the model the bigger chunk around the match (enough context).

In Lab 3 Equation 16.19 matched our Lab 1 loop on every page. In the exercise, no overlap at all scored higher than overlap on these questions: no rule replaces measuring.

The same RAG with LangChain

In Lab 4 three defaults raised no error. Listing 16.2 as written counts characters, not tokens: 408 chunks of ≈ 115 tokens instead of 141. OpenAIEmbeddings sends token ids, which OpenRouter rejects with a 400 unless check_embedding_ctx_length=False. And EnsembleRetriever only merges what each retriever returns: 3 per list gave 13/20, 60 per list gave 17/20. We caught all three because we knew what was inside and measured every time.

One question missed every method: the analogy question asked longer, with the author’s name and the book’s title. The library-card chunk was at rank 8 with dense, 5 with hybrid, and 17 with parent-child. The words in the question pull it towards other pages; the fix, transforming the question before searching, is in EP11.

Review questions

  1. Which of the three limits does a book published after the model was trained show, and how does RAG get around it?
  2. A team wants answers that always follow the faculty’s report format. RAG, fine-tuning or long context, and why?
  3. Why does car·banana scoring 0.58 not break top-k?
  4. Why is “the documents don’t contain this” a good answer from a RAG system?
  5. Give a question BM25 answers better than dense retrieval, and one where dense wins.
  6. Why does RRF use ranks instead of adding the two scores?
  7. Parent-child got the best hit@3 in Lab 3. What does it trade for that?
  8. In Lab 4, what did changing 3 per list to 60 per list change, and why didn’t LangChain warn us?
